UNCHARTED

AI built on Meta's Muse models has beaten 5 world records in mathematics.

We built an AI research team. It invents its own methods, writes the code, tests it, studies pictures of its results, and keeps going until it finds a better answer than the best one mathematicians have ever published.

The story in 30 seconds

1
The challenge

Spread a number of points over a sphere so that no two are close together. For 150+ points in four dimensions, nobody knows the best arrangement. Mathematicians keep a public table of the best answers found so far.

2
The AI writes code

Muse Spark invents a method and writes a program to search for a better arrangement. Eight attempts run at once.

3
It checks, then looks

Each program runs in a safe sandbox and a strict checker scores the answer. The AI then studies a picture of the result to see what to fix next.

4
A lead AI steers

Muse Wonder reviews all attempts every few rounds, works out why progress stalled, and assigns new strategies.

5
A new world record

When an answer beats the published best, we recheck it independently with 50-digit arithmetic.

Why it matters: this geometry decides how well signals can be told apart, so it sits underneath radio, Wi-Fi and error-correcting codes. And it shows AI doing real research: finding results nobody had found before.

How do we know it's real?

You don't have to trust us. Every claim can be checked by anyone in a few minutes.

1
The published best

Each record we beat is listed in Henry Cohn's public table (MIT), the standard reference for this problem.

2
Our coordinates

The exact positions of every point are public. Download them below and compute the score yourself.

3
A strict, independent check

A separate program recomputes every pair of points with 50-digit arithmetic, far beyond normal computer precision. The improvements are more than 100,000 times larger than any rounding error.

4
Run it yourself

python tools/verify_code.py records/spherical_4d_292/code_4_292.csv 0.903471504338

Questions judges ask

Are these really new results?

Yes. Each one beats the value listed in the public table at the time we checked it, and the coordinates are public so anyone can confirm. We are submitting them to the table's maintainer for official listing.

Did the AI start from scratch?

No, and we say so openly. For the sphere records, the AI starts from the best published arrangement and searches for a different, better one nearby. Simple local polishing of the published answers gives no improvement at all (we tested it); the AI had to invent ways to rearrange the points. In our warm-up test (26 circles in a square) it started from scratch and beat Google DeepMind's published AlphaEvolve result, though not the current best.

The improvements look tiny. Do they count?

Yes. In this table the competition is at the 6th–12th decimal place, and any verified improvement replaces the old record. Ours are around 0.0000001–0.0000008, more than 100,000 times larger than rounding error.

Why does this problem matter?

Spreading points apart on a sphere is the geometry behind telling signals apart: it appears in radio and Wi-Fi signal design, error-correcting codes and data compression. It is also a classic open problem with a long history.

What did the humans do?

We built the lab: the sandbox, the checker, the pictures and the loop that connects the models. The methods that broke the records were written by Muse Spark, guided by Muse Wonder.

Can it work on other problems?

Yes. A new problem needs only three pieces: a description, a checker and a picture. The same lab ran circle packing and sphere arrangements without changing the engine.
